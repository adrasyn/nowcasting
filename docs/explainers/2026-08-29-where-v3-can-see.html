Where v3 Can See

The model can't reach back before 2021, shouldn't be trusted before 2023, and produced a wild answer for 2022. Here is why, in plain English.

29 August 2026 Based on 146 model fits run 28 August Written for someone coming to this fresh

The whole thing in four points

The wall
One data feed — online job ads — only starts in January 2021. The model refuses to run without it. That sets a hard floor at May 2021.
The 2022 blowup
Not a bug. The model is built with a special dial for the lockdown period, and in 2022 it is still steering by that. It predicts the COVID rebound one quarter after the rebound ended.
The good news
From 2023 onward the machinery is stable and cheap to run. Every reliability test passed.
The bad news, and it's the real one
In the years where it is stable, v3 says almost the same number every time — around 0.6% — no matter what the data does. It barely beats simply guessing the long-run average.

First, the machine

What the model is doing, in one minute

The model watches 14 Australian data series — jobs, exports, building approvals, business surveys, prices. Most of them come out monthly. GDP comes out quarterly, months late. The job is to guess the GDP number before the ABS publishes it.

It works by assuming a few hidden trends sit underneath everything. A "general economy" trend, a "labour market" trend, a "prices" trend. Each visible series is a noisy reflection of one or more of them. If exports and job ads both jump, the general trend probably moved, and GDP probably moved with it.

Those hidden trends can't be measured directly. They have to be estimated. And that estimation is where the strange behaviour comes from.

The bit you asked about

Warm and cold

The model isn't solved with a formula. It's solved by trial and error, at scale. The computer starts with a guess for every hidden trend, then nudges everything thousands of times until the numbers stop moving. Statisticians call each nudge a sweep.

Here's the problem: where it starts changes where it ends up.

Picture a marble on a hilly landscape. Drop it and it rolls downhill into a valley. But there are two valleys. One is the right answer, where GDP moves with the monthly data. The other is a wrong answer, where the model decides GDP just does its own thing and ignores everything else — that one still produces a confident-looking number, and it's meaningless.

So: cold means starting from a blank, neutral guess. Warm means starting from last month's finished answer. Since a month of new data barely changes the picture, last month's answer is already very close to this month's — the marble is placed in the right valley instead of dropped from the sky.

Two things surprised me when I measured it.

First, warm isn't faster per sweep. It's identical. What warm saves is having to retry. A cold run that lands in the wrong valley has to be thrown away and re-run with a different random start, and there's no way to know in advance which will work. Six cold runs took eleven attempts between them.

Second, there is no lucky number you can reuse. A random start that works at one date fails at the next. We test them; the one that worked in 2022 failed at three later dates. So a cold-started backtest re-runs that lottery at every single date. That is the real cost, and warm removes it entirely.

Why this matters practically

A warm run takes 28 seconds. The equivalent cold run, including its failed attempts, took 21 minutes at one date. That's why we can now afford to test the model across the whole usable history instead of picking a short window.

Problem one

The data is younger than you'd think

Australia's official statistics go back a long way — GDP itself starts in 1959. But the model can only start when its newest series starts, because it refuses to run with a series that hasn't begun yet. That's deliberate: a blank row isn't missing data the model can work around, it's a series that doesn't exist.

Three data problems sit behind that picture

The ABS keeps killing monthly series. Five have been discontinued since March 2025 — retail trade, the monthly CPI indicator, business turnover, payroll jobs, and employee earnings. A dead series doesn't announce itself. It just stops updating, and looks fine until you check the dates.

Monthly inflation is split in half. The live ABS catalogue only publishes monthly CPI back to April 2024. The rest — back to September 2017 — sits in a closed catalogue that will never update again. We now stitch the two together, which is why the CPI bar above reaches 2017 and not 2024.

One series couldn't be stitched, so we removed it. There was a second inflation measure (the "trimmed mean"). The old catalogue only ever published it as a percentage rate, not as an index, and you can't join a rate onto an index. It was stuck at April 2024, and being stuck, it was setting the floor — no test could start before July 2024. Dropping it moved the floor back to May 2021 and roughly tripled the testable history. That was yesterday's change.

Problem two, and it's the interesting one

Why 2022 comes out so huge

The short answer: COVID is still in the model's field of view, and in 2022 it is most of what the model can see.

Look at Australian quarterly GDP growth. Normal quarters move by a few tenths of a percent. The lockdown quarters move by multiples of that — one of them by nearly seven percent.

The model was given a dedicated dial to absorb that period — an extra hidden trend that is switched on only between March 2020 and December 2021, and off everywhere else. Without it, the lockdown quarters would distort everything.

But that dial creates its own problem. Because COVID is such a huge share of GDP's total movement, the dial is doing most of the work of explaining GDP. And when you ask the model to predict a quarter in early 2022, it is standing right at the edge of that window, with barely any post-COVID data to learn from. The most recent thing it has seen is the biggest economic shock in the series.

So it predicts the rebound, one quarter late

Average miss in 2022: 2.1 percentage points. For a number that normally lives between 0% and 1%, that is not a forecast, it's noise.

The practical conclusion

Don't test the model on 2022. It isn't a fair test — it is asking the model to work in the one window where its own design guarantees it can't. I'd start the evaluation at January 2023. That costs four quarters out of twenty and removes a source of noise that would tell us nothing.

The part I think you're reacting to

Even where it works, it barely says anything

Once you leave 2022 behind, the model stops producing crazy numbers. The misses shrink a lot.

2.12average miss in 2022, percentage points
0.45average miss in 2024
0.28average miss in 2026

That looks like progress, and in one narrow sense it is: on the Q1 2026 quarter that started this whole project — where v2 called +0.77% against an outcome near +0.3%, a miss of about half a point — v3 missed by about 0.30. Better, on that one quarter.

But look at how it gets there.

The fair way to judge that is against the dumbest possible alternatives. Two of them: "assume this quarter matches last quarter," and "assume this quarter matches the ten-year average."

So your instinct was right. The reliability results are genuinely good; the usefulness results are not, yet. A nowcast that answers "about 0.6%" regardless of the incoming data isn't reading the data.

Why might that be? The most likely reason is the same COVID problem, one level deeper. Because those eight quarters hold 91% of GDP's movement, fitting GDP is mostly an exercise in fitting COVID. The link between GDP and the monthly indicators — which is the entire point of a nowcast — ends up weak, even in the "right valley". The model isn't broken. It's being drowned out.

Caveats, stated plainly

What this does and doesn't prove

  • These runs weren't designed as an accuracy test. They were designed to measure cost and stability. The accuracy read above is a bonus I extracted afterwards, on ten predictions across four quarters. That's a hint, not a result.
  • The comparison flatters v3. The model was fed today's revised figures, not the figures that existed at the time. Real forecasting is harder than this. A proper test needs the original data as first published.
  • "Miss" is measured against revised GDP too — the ABS itself revises these numbers, sometimes substantially.
  • The reliability findings are solid. 60 out of 60, and 28 out of 75, are large enough to trust.

Where I'd go next

Three things, in order

1. Run the full test properly, 2023 onward

Now affordable — about an hour and a half of compute for every quarter we can reach. That turns the ten-prediction hint above into a real answer, scored against v2 and against the dumb benchmarks on the same terms.

2. Deal with the COVID problem directly

This is the root cause of both the 2022 blowup and the flat-line behaviour. Options exist — shorten the window the special dial covers, start the model's history after COVID entirely, or handle those quarters differently. Each is a real change with real trade-offs, and it is the question worth spending time on.

3. Only then, look at the panel

The original theory was that v2 was blind to commodity and survey signals. v3 added those. But if GDP's link to all the monthly data is weak, adding more series won't help — the problem is upstream of which series we picked.

The one-line version

The plumbing is now fast, reliable, and honest about its limits. The model is not yet earning its keep, and the reason looks structural rather than a matter of tuning.
